A Reflection on "The Risk of Nuclear Conflict Is Rising" by W.J. Hennigan

W.J. Hennigan wrote this piece for the New York Times and it is about nuclear weapons, but not in the way I expected. It is not really about the history of them or the politics behind them, it is more about what would actually happen if one went off today. Not a giant bomb like the ones dropped on Japan, but a smaller tactical weapon, something that fits inside an artillery shell and is considered more "usable" in a war like the one happening in Ukraine right now. It goes through the blast, the fires, the radiation sickness that shows up days or even weeks later, the food shortages spreading to other continents from contaminated crops and agricultural shutdowns. What I kept coming back to while reading was how little I actually knew about any of this. I try to follow the news and have a decent understanding of what is happening in the war in Ukraine. But I had no idea that in fall 2022 American intelligence was putting the odds at roughly 50-50 that Russia would launch a nuclear weapon to stop Ukrainian forces from pushing into Crimea.

The article also spends a lot of time on deterrence and what it means. The basic idea is that countries hold back because they know the other side will destroy them if they attack first. What I missed was that deterrence only really functions when there is actual communication happening, countries sharing real information about what weapons they have and where they are. There is a room in the State Department set up exactly for this, a direct line to Russia that used to handle over a thousand messages a year about weapons movements and tests. After Russia pulled out of the last major arms treaty that number dropped to fewer than a dozen. That detail stayed with me more than anything else. It made me think about how in my own life when communication stops, even in smaller situations, things start going wrong fast because everyone is just guessing about what the other person means or wants, and usually guessing in the most anxious direction possible.

I am someone who spends a lot of time thinking about images and how they shape the way people see the world, and this article connected to that in a way I did not expect going in. When something gets shown over and over it starts losing its weight somehow, it just becomes part of the background. Ukraine has been in the news for years and somewhere in that time I stopped really registering it as something urgent and started just kind of filing it away. Reading this made me feel like I had been looking at the situation like a photograph of something, getting the surface of it without asking what was actually going on behind it.
